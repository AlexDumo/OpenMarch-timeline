Move priority cases

When two moves overlap for the same marcher, one has to win. Below are two ways to decide, and ten cases that try to break them. Each animation is computed by a small simulator using the spec's rules: the winning move controls the marcher, and when a move picks a marcher back up it starts from where they are and finishes on time.

Two ways to decide who wins

ALast edited on top

Every time you make, drag or resize a move, it goes on top of whatever it overlaps. Who wins depends on the order you did things in, which the timeline doesn't show.

BLater start on top

A page's own moves are always underneath. Between your moves, the one that starts later takes over, so a marcher follows a move until the next one picks them up. If two start on the same count, the shorter one wins, so moves nest like brackets. Who wins depends only on what's on the timeline.

What the cases say

A surprises whenever edit history matters. That covers nudging a move, moving a page flag, making moves in a different order, and two moves starting together. In those cases a move can end up fully hidden: it's still on the timeline but never plays.

B never lets one of your moves hide another. To hide a move, another would need the same start and a later end, and then it would be the longer one, so it loses. Several shorter moves inside a long one can still cover all of it, and the faded counts show that. Who wins changes only when starts visibly swap on the timeline. Case 6 is the closest to a surprise: making a long move over a short one keeps the short one as a breakaway. That's intended; the designer decides whether to keep the inner move.

Both can make marchers sprint when a move hands them back to a page's move with little time left (case 7). That isn't a layer problem; it needs a warning.

With B, nobody picks a layer, including the app's code. The layer module works layers out from the timeline, and a check confirms the stored layers always match. That's the "enforced" part.
